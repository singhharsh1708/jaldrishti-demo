Method, data and what to trust

JalDrishti couples the Greater Chennai Corporation storm-water drain survey with the streets above it in one differentiable model. It forecasts water depth on every street segment every 10 minutes, ranks drains for desilting in a single adjoint pass, and works back from a few street-depth reports to find silted drains. This page explains how, and where the prototype stops.

How it works

  1. Drain network from the GCC survey

    Each surveyed drain comes with its route, inverts, size and condition. Drains are split and snapped into a graph wherever they meet, within m. The prototype site covers wards (zones ): junctions and drain segments from surveyed drains. Drain junctions within m of an OpenStreetMap waterway (canal, river, drain or stream) are outfalls ( on the site).

  2. Street surface and flow

    Street level comes from the Copernicus GLO-30 DEM, anchored so that the median drain has m of cover. Each street is modelled as a 1D channel, m wide, along its drain. It is not a 2D grid. With a 30 m DEM, the street surface has closed low points where water pools: of junctions sit lower than every neighbour, and all junctions where street depth passes 1 m in any scenario lie in closed depressions. Treat depths over 1 m as model artifacts. Flow in drains and streets is local-inertial (Bates et al. 2010), with a Preissmann-style slot so drains can surcharge. Water crosses between street and drain through inlets as weir or orifice flow (Chen et al. 2007) in both directions, so a surcharged drain pushes water back onto the street. Silt raises a drain's bed and cuts its capacity. The model is written in JAX and is differentiable end to end.

  3. Storm scenarios

    Four synthetic storms of mm over minutes, bell-shaped in time with the peak at minute , and the same everywhere on the site. Peak rates: mm/h. Time step s, depths saved every minutes. Each storm runs with clean drains, with the silted drains of the twin test, and with the silt inferred from gauges: runs in all. A segment's depth is the larger of its two end-junction street depths.

  4. Desilting priority from one adjoint pass

    Reverse-mode differentiation gives the gradient of street flood volume above 15 cm with respect to silt in every drain segment at once, in s for the mm storm. Checked against brute-force re-runs, one per segment ( s): Spearman rank correlation , and of the top 20 segments agree. The ranking sums the linear estimate per surveyed drain; those per-drain sums were not re-checked by silting whole drains. Summing the single-segment brute-force effects per drain instead gives Spearman and of the top 20 drains in common, over all drains. The Desilting list shows both numbers for each drain, and they disagree sharply for some.

  5. Finding silted drains from street reports (twin test)

    In each run, silt is planted in drains of a "truth" model. Simulated gauges read street depth every 10 minutes with cm noise, and the model estimates one silt value per candidate drain ( candidates, picked by a brute-force screen of the clean model; the planted drains are always drawn from these candidates). Over runs each, the four highest estimates held of planted drains with 10 gauges and of with 30, against about by random picking. Street-depth error at flooded junctions with no gauge in a different storm fell from cm with 10 gauges and from cm with 30 (mean absolute peak depth).

  6. Flood-safe routing

    Dijkstra over the drain-street segments runs in the browser. Speed on a flooded segment follows the depth-disruption curve of Pregnolato et al. (2017), v = 0.0009 w² − 0.5529 w + 86.9448 km/h for depth w in mm, capped at km/h. Segments with cm of water or more are closed, and every metre of closed street carries a penalty: m of it outweighs driving the whole network. The safe route therefore avoids closed streets whenever any open route exists, then takes the least time. When every route crosses a closed street, the page says so and shows the least flooded route instead: the deepest water on it is as shallow as possible, then it crosses the least length of closed street, then it takes the least time. That route is not safe, and it can cross more closed street than the shortest one. The Route API in api/ applies the same rules over HTTP.

        Reproduce

        From the repository root: the export writes the flood scenarios, desilting priority and twin result to site/data/; the nowcast writes the radar frames and scores there; the bundle step packs site/data/ into site/data.js. Then open site/index.html directly, or serve site/ with a static server such as npx http-server site -p 8000. Python's built-in http.server can drop one of the page's parallel requests and leave a blank map.

        PYTHONPATH=. .venv/bin/python -m jaldrishti.export_web
PYTHONPATH=. .venv/bin/python -m jaldrishti.nowcast
PYTHONPATH=. .venv/bin/python -m jaldrishti.evidence
PYTHONPATH=. .venv/bin/python -m jaldrishti.rain2015
PYTHONPATH=. .venv/bin/python -m jaldrishti.validate
PYTHONPATH=. .venv/bin/python -m jaldrishti.calibrate
PYTHONPATH=. .venv/bin/python -m jaldrishti.swmm_check

open site/index.html

        By default the nowcast uses a fixed window of scans (--until in jaldrishti/nowcast.py), the window behind the published radar numbers. --until latest uses the whole archive and changes every radar number; re-check the Radar tab and the video after it.

        The page reads everything from site/data.js, so it works from disk and from any static host. Rebuild the bundle after re-running the export or the nowcast.
